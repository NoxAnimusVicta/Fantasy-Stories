# Magic and worship in the western passes

At Gannet the crown's school occupies two rooms above the scales. In the first room, pupils sit with a copper wire between finger and thumb. The master bends the far end under a cloth. A pupil who can feel the movement through the wire learns to send a small movement back. Many spend a term there. The crown pays board for pupils who sign for three years of station service. Others bring their own food. A labourer's child can have the necessary gift and still lose the place when their family needs another wage.

The second room holds the speaking stone paired to Tallowmarket's quay station. Both desks are staffed at dawn and sundown. The operator at Gannet, Mere Ott, trained at public expense. Her reserve is small. She can carry a short message cleanly on the first attempt. A watch officer or guildmaster can send urgent official traffic against their seal. A private sender pays one Silver Mark for an available turn, about a labourer's day wage, and the receiving clerk must still find the addressee. Public traffic takes precedence. Both clerks record the sender, wording and receipt. A blank receipt means nobody confirmed hearing it. Reed has no paired stone. At Crow Lodge news still comes up the bench on someone's feet.

At Red Well, Beska teaches young riders to carry pressure through a rein without pulling a tired mare's mouth. Her grandchildren practise on a leather loop tied to a tent peg before they are trusted with a horse. She can pick out a lost calf through a familiar dog's reactions. A stranger's dog gives her very little. The royal examiner once offered to put her down as an assistant beast handler. She sent him the fee for examining his own mother. Beska teaches relatives and a few pupils vouched for at the winter fire. A Red Well orphan who grew up in town could find it easier to enter the crown school than to win that introduction.

Dorr Kett's gate runes tell him which marked joints have moved. He examines the stone to learn why. An unmarked timber pier offers him no warning. Gannet's guild counts Alis Fen as a warrior because she sets her mana in the long hook she uses to lift casualties. The hook stays rigid under a twisting load. She still needs three other people to carry a grown dwarf down the bench. Neither practitioner can substitute for Mere at the desk without learning her craft.

## Burial, recognition and pay

The Lamp chapel at Gannet keeps the names of billet households. Supper for mourners comes out of its kitchen. At Reed, boat hands pour the Storm Father's share of a ferry wage into the water when a crew member drowns. The Gatekeeper's mortuary at Gannet has a cold stone room, a wash bench and a clerk employed jointly by temple and pay office. When the river brings a body down, the clerk records the recovery place, identifying marks and possessions. A recognisable face and a witness who knew the person allow the priest to enter a name. An uncertain identification stays under a description. Families may inspect the effects. Claimants cannot take them away before the entry is copied.

A company officer certifies service and the last paid muster. For a named dead soldier the pay clerk matches that certificate to the mortuary entry and releases arrears to the recorded dependant. Where there is no body, two named witnesses to the death can give sworn accounts. A missing soldier's family can draw the district dependant ration while the case stays open. A widow whose husband vanished on the inland road may have food and still wait months for his wages. Kharadi casualties travel with a clan witness and their own pay roll. A human priest's signature gives a dwarven family no authority over a clan account.

At Crow Lodge a halfling household sets aside the dead person's last loaf until a relative comes for it. In summer they dry slices to keep it. At Red Well the shaman names the dead beside the ancestral fire and hears anybody who rode with them. Gannet's clerk accepts such a witness under the same muster rule. The surety required of an orc after dark has nevertheless left witnesses waiting outside until morning while bodies were carried away for burial. The clerk's clean entries conceal what happened at the gate.

Sister Anet of the Gate can hold lesser dead outside a threshold while she keeps vigil. She cannot watch the chapel door and the mortuary at once. In the panic after the causeways fell, the bailiff ordered the hands bound on every unidentified corpse. One ferryman's children found the cord marks when they came to wash him. Anet cut the remaining cords in front of the watch. Nothing has risen here. A blessing provides a farewell and a witness.
