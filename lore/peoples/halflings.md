# Halflings

Halflings are small, hardy people who favour good soil, dry hillsides and a village within walking distance of their cousins. Their oldest homes are dug into south-facing banks. A visitor comes in through a round front door at ground level and finds a kitchen bright with windows, bedrooms farther into the hill and a cool pantry whose back wall never sees the sun. Where the ground floods, halflings build above it. Their attachment to a dry bed is quite as strong as their affection for custom.

## The village and the moot

Households own their homes and cultivated strips. Grazing, woodland rights, the millpond and the lanes between fields belong to the village in common. At a moot each household sends someone empowered to give its word. A reeve chosen there can settle a boundary quarrel or call out the watch. Selling common land requires the households themselves to agree. A human baron once thought he had bought a hill outright when the reeve sold him its stone. The quarrymen were still arguing with gooseherds about access when the baron died.

Several villages can send reeves to a larger moot to settle disputes or act together. Halflings living under a human crown guard these rights closely. Their charters often promise scouts, slingers or provisions in wartime. One village's oath gives the king little claim on its neighbours.

## Kin, guests and wanderers

Halfling households tend to be large. A young couple often add rooms to a family hill until they can establish a place of their own. The household shrine stands by the oven, where the first loaf of a baking is broken for the hearth powers. At funerals relatives cook the dishes for which the deceased was known. Being able to reproduce an aunt's seed cake can earn more praise at her wake than the finest eulogy.

The custom of offering supper and a bed to a traveller makes halflings unusually well informed about the roads. Peddlers, tinkers and adventurers carry introductions from one village to the next. Some spend their lives travelling and still claim a particular seat at the kitchen table back home. Guildhouses value halflings who can take a frightened party cross-country and find a friendly door at the other end.

## The watch

Village watches favour slings, short bows and keen dogs. The best scouts grow up slipping between hedges and moving quietly among livestock. On campaign they guide columns, carry dispatches and discover which bridges will bear a wagon. At home they can muster every able household to defend a lane, but a halfling village has little chance of holding open country against an undead host.

Refugee households often arrive together, with the reeve carrying the charter and somebody's grandfather carrying the sourdough starter. They look for land on which the village can settle together. A host ruler who offers individual tenancies spread across an estate may be surprised when the whole company shoulders its packs and walks on.
