# Humans

Across the human kingdoms a traveller recognises three buildings from the road. The keep stands on the defensible height, the temple has the tallest bell, and the town wall has been extended at least once. In prosperous country the houses have already spilled beyond it again. Human settlements grow quickly enough for a mason to lay a gate's foundations as an apprentice and take it down as master to make room for a larger town.

## Crowns, land and allegiance

Most human crowns descend through families. A sovereign grants estates in return for armed service. A lesser knight might owe one horse and forty days in the field. A great lord may owe hundreds of spears and own the only bridge by which they can reach the muster. Chartered towns bargain directly with the crown for the right to elect officers, raise walls and keep a militia. The magistrate and the castellan can both produce genuine royal charters when they quarrel over who commands the town gate.

Vardane's northern lords grew powerful keeping frontier castles. A crown that stripped them of their soldiers would have to defend the frontier itself. Cyranne, farther south, can keep household regiments under arms between campaigns. Its sovereign still needs the estates to finance a long war abroad. These are two human answers to the same royal problem. A king can inherit the loyalty pledged to his father and still have to persuade the men who must saddle up and ride.

## Households and faith

A noble household raises pages, squires, foster children and poor cousins alongside its heirs. Those childhood ties later cross the lines of armies. Most people grow up closer to home, learning a parent's trade or entering service in another household. Inheritance makes younger children a steady source of apprentices, soldiers, clergy and adventurers. A guildhouse offers something precious to a cooper's third daughter who can handle a spear. Her rank travels with her.

Human temples keep local saints. A miller might pray to the same gods as his sovereign and still entrust his daughter on the road to a woman hanged centuries ago for sheltering fugitives. Her little roadside shrines each have a dry shelf for tinder. A knight swears before witnesses and relics. Breaking such an oath can cost his heirs good marriages long after he is dead.

## War

Knights, household foot, town militias and hired companies fight under distinct obligations. A levy can hold its own wall with extraordinary tenacity and go home when ordered to campaign through harvest. Northern commanders prize veterans who know how to bring a company back in order. The living have to carry their wounded, recover their dead and fight tomorrow with whoever comes home.

In the invasion's eighth year a Vardanian lord can still hold a legal title to an estate behind the undead lines. Some such lords lead their surviving tenants in exile. Others press claims to houses already occupied by refugees. Their heraldry affords a stranger little clue which sort has just ridden through the gate.
