# Magic and faith

## Mana and the people who learn to use it

Every living person has a reserve of mana. It can grow with sustained practice. Capacity governs how much a person can spend before exhaustion. Control governs where the expenditure goes, how long it holds its shape and how much is wasted in getting it there. A pupil with a deep reserve can afford a hundred poor attempts. A pupil with little to spare may learn to do the work in one pulse and still have enough left to walk home. In a contest the first pupil can keep spending after a mistake. The second has every reason to arrange matters so the first mistake belongs to somebody else.

Most people have so little readily accessible mana that their first deliberate working takes patient instruction. A carter who can earn a living at once seldom has the months or the teacher. People born with moderate or deep reserves often feel the response clearly enough to repeat it alone. A self-taught prodigy may discover an extraordinary knack and know very little outside it. A great reserve makes rapid learning and extravagant techniques possible. It grants neither judgment nor familiarity with somebody else's working.

The river crowns fund schools because they want operators, artificers and battlefield practitioners. Clan hearths, herding families and tribal teachers keep other methods alive through apprenticeship. An institutional certificate may get a caster through a city gate while the best teacher of their particular art lives three weeks away under canvas. Neither ancestry nor admission to a school decides what a person can learn.

### What an adventurer means by a speciality

Warrior, ranger and mage are answers people give over a guild supper. Rank is a separate matter. A Copper mage works under a sponsor just as a Copper warrior does.

| Common description | What another adventurer expects to see |
|---|---|
| Warrior | Enhancement carried close through flesh, armour or a weapon. One fighter holds reinforcement through a long march. Another spends it for the instant an axe bites and has none running a heartbeat later. |
| Ranger | Concealment, ranged work, trained beasts, decoys or articulated puppets. A herder might carry a familiar signal to a hound. A quarry scout might guide a jointed wooden bird around a ledge by a fine line of mana. Each has learned a different trade. |
| Mage | Enough control over complex workings to shape effects beyond the body. Many spend their training on this and are vulnerable when caught close. Some are spectacular. A specialist in stilling vibrations might do their best work in complete silence. |

A watcher sees where mana is being used and what the working does. An enhanced archer could reasonably give either of the first two answers. A mage can reinforce a staff or guide a decoy and pass for either until they show more. Few practising warriors or rangers can produce the elaborate sustained working that makes observers confident they have seen a mage. Clothes, weapons and the size of a reserve settle little.

A blood mage gathering the blood of the fallen into a dragon above a field has to hold its changing shape and direction while it moves. A blood warrior drawing blood inward to sustain a mana-driven surge of circulation and oxygenation spends their craft on their own body. The common material gives the names their first word. What each does with it gives the second. Moving blood confers no dominion over a corpse or its soul.

## What a practitioner can do

A trained mage can learn several workings over a lifetime. Lighting a wet hearth, drawing heat from a feverish body and casting fire across a courtyard all involve heat. Competence at one gives a pupil a beginning in the others. The ability to aim a flame past one's own shieldmen takes practice that a village healer seldom gets.

Large workings require something that holds the spell's shape once the caster's attention moves on. Dwarven runes can warn a gatekeeper when a marked joint shifts. Elven wardens tend living boundaries over generations. Human artificers set enchantments into tools, weapons and matched speaking stones. Two staffed stations can exchange short dispatches over their usual link. If the far operator has fled, no answer comes back. Common enchantments have makers who can teach the craft and workshops that can repair them. The Lich King alone made the three artifacts that confer his dominion.

## Temples and miracles

Miracles occur. A priest at work draws on living mana. Whether a god answers through the act is a question the temples answer differently. Knowing a rite's motions does not confer its mastery or the standing to officiate. At a sickbed a sworn healer can sometimes close a wound that stitching would never hold. Priests of the Gate can turn the lesser dead from a threshold while they keep vigil there. A priest who leaves to help at another door takes that protection along. A bishop's title establishes who appoints the temple officers. It tells a soldier very little about who can hold the chapel steps.

In the human river kingdoms, the Lady of the Lamp receives household prayers, the Gatekeeper attends burial and the Storm Father is invoked under open skies. Individual temples preserve local saints and choose which feasts matter most to their communities. A roadside saint can be dearer to a particular regiment than its sovereign's patron. The Gatekeeper's clergy teach that the soul passes beyond their reach at death. The dead King's magic has given them ample evidence about bodies and very little about where those bodies' former inhabitants have gone.

Dwarven clans swear before their ancestors. Orc shamans keep the ancestral fires and interpret dreams before a gathering of warbands. An elf seeking the favour of a river power visits that particular river. Halfling hearth rites pass within households, often through the person who teaches the children to bake. Visitors can witness a working or share in a rite without acquiring the right to officiate. These traditions have their own accounts of the dead.

## Death and spellcasting

The living peoples know rites that detect disturbed graves, protect a place for a time and lay individual restless spirits to rest. Ordinary funeral blessing gives mourners no guarantee against the Rime Scythe. Breaking a Royal Seal remains a physical achievement worth the journey.

A dead captain raised from a mage remembers the craft and can recognise its work. A living caster draws on breath and pulse to shape a new spell. The Crown gives a corpse motion and command but restores neither faculty. Such a captain can identify a speaking stone and order its operator taken. He cannot replace that operator. A device with a completed enchantment and a mechanical trigger can still be used by hands capable of moving it. The King's own spellcraft runs through the artifacts he built for his transformation.
