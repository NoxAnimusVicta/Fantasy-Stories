# On foot above Lime End

The bench is a shelf in the north wall of a glacial valley. Stand at Lime End facing upstream and the river is on your right. The pack track climbs away to your left. On the opposite bank you can see cut hay and a line of alders. That apparently gentler way ends against a cliff where the river has eaten the bank. The men who mow there bring their scythes across by punt.

```text
west  Lime End — kiln — quarry floor — White Scar — Crow Lodge    east

                            \ lower spring path      /   | Seed Shelf
                             \ alder pool — wet lip /    | farm track
       ← main river       gorge and south-bank cliff    headwater bog
```

Distances mark the bench trail from the wagon yard. The ridge and the lower path reconnect with it. The diagram shows landmarks in travel order. A party following the riverbed cannot walk through the gorge.

**Kiln wood, kilometres 0–8.** Grey stumps in straight rows betray the former coppice. Brambles grow high enough to hide its charcoal pits. The kiln sheds have tile roofs, open sides and standing room for a dozen. The abandoned shed with a pear tree growing through it belongs to the sisters who cut broom handles here. They leave the latch up for travellers. Take their seasoned billets for firewood and the latch goes down for everybody. From this floor the rim hides Crow Lodge and the entire north road.

**Quarry, kilometres 8–15.** Cart ruts taper into a footpath at the old lime face. The quarry floor holds rainwater fouled by sheep. The first dependable water on the pack route comes from a spring in the spoil at kilometre eleven. In frost the trough skins over first and the spring mouth runs beneath its timber lid. The tool house sleeps four dry. Its outer wall still carries the heights of children whose parents worked the quarry. One family came back last summer to cut a grown son's name above his last mark.

**White Scar, kilometres 15–25.** At the lip above the quarry one can see the nest ledge and the lodge roof when the air is clear. The hollow directly below the nest disappears from view. A sheep dragged there can be heard from the ridge and seen only from below. The ridge walk is passable on foot in dry weather. The steep return to the bench at kilometre twenty-five defeats a laden mule. The lower spring path skirts the nesting ledge, adds four hours to the through journey and crosses a wet stone lip. In thaw the lip goes underwater. A guide waiting for that path to clear uses the quarry shelter and consumes another day's provisions.

**Upper bench, kilometres 25–32.** The second trail spring issues under an overhang at kilometre twenty-six. Its outlet sometimes freezes solid. The overhang shelters two seated people and drips at the back. Beyond it the trail leaves bare stone for the strip of thorn hedge planted to keep goats off Seed Shelf. When someone sees furrows through the hedge they are within an hour of the lodge. A track of hard cart width here connects terrace farms to their own granary. Its west end is a threshing floor overlooking a drop. The nearest through wagon road is still back at Lime End.

**Off the bench.** Seed Shelf grows hulled oats, keeps the best dry stalls for milk cows and cuts hay on ground too wet to plough. The bog above it feeds the river. Red deer bed on its western fringe in summer and descend to the kiln wood after snow. Old Senn's sheep graze over the watershed. His dogs escort sheep towards his fold when storms break. A traveller following their tracks can cross into Red Well country without ever seeing Keld. In deep snow the two springs and secure footing determine whether a winter delivery goes out. The ten windows in the economic account already allow for lost days. Closing both water points cancels the trip.
